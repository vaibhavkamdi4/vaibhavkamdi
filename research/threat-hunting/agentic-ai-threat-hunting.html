Agentic AI for Threat Hunting:
From Intelligence to Evidence

A public-safe overview of how AI-assisted automation can help threat hunters move from intelligence review and hunt preparation to SIEM investigation, evidence collection, and structured reporting—without exposing proprietary implementation details.

1. Introduction

Threat hunters often work across threat intelligence reports, SIEM platforms, detection rules, investigation evidence, and incident documentation. These activities require significant manual effort, especially when translating adversary intelligence into practical hunting hypotheses and repeatable investigations.

An Agentic AI-assisted Threat Hunting Platform explores how these activities can be streamlined through intelligent analysis, SIEM integration, evidence collection, and structured reporting.

The objective is not to replace the threat hunter. It is to reduce repetitive work, improve investigation consistency, and help analysts focus on validating hypotheses and interpreting security evidence.

2. The challenge for modern threat hunters

Threat hunting involves more than searching for known indicators. Analysts must understand adversary behaviour, identify relevant telemetry, develop hypotheses, construct queries, validate results, and document their findings.

  • Manually extracting IOCs and relevant techniques from threat intelligence reports.
  • Translating intelligence into practical SIEM queries and detection logic.
  • Repeating similar searches across investigations.
  • Correlating search results with threat hypotheses.
  • Capturing evidence for peer review and incident documentation.
  • Producing consistent investigation reports under time constraints.

Automation can assist with these activities, provided that its outputs remain traceable and subject to analyst validation.

3. Platform overview

The platform is designed to support a threat-hunting investigation from intelligence analysis through evidence-backed reporting. At a capability level, it brings together five areas:

Threat intelligence analysis

Extracts relevant information from adversary reports, including summaries, indicators, artefacts, and potential behaviours.

Hunt preparation and detection assistance

Helps translate available intelligence into hunting hypotheses, SIEM queries, and Sigma detection rules for analyst review.

SIEM-based investigation

Supports the execution of hunting queries against Splunk and the review of returned telemetry.

Evidence collection

Captures browser-based evidence to support reproducibility, investigation review, and documentation.

Investigation reporting

Consolidates available findings, IOC candidates, executed queries, search results, and screenshot evidence into a PDF report.

Public disclosure boundary: This overview describes capabilities and analyst-facing outcomes. It intentionally omits internal implementation details, proprietary orchestration, prompts, source code, and internal processing logic.

5. How it supports a threat-hunting investigation

Stage 1 — Intelligence review

Threat intelligence provides context about adversary infrastructure, known indicators, observed behaviours, and relevant techniques. Automated extraction can help analysts identify useful information more quickly, reducing the effort required to review lengthy reports manually.

Stage 2 — Hunt preparation

Extracted intelligence can inform hunting hypotheses and candidate queries. The analyst should review the assumptions, data sources, query logic, and time range before executing a hunt. Generated Sigma rules should also be validated against the intended telemetry and detection requirements.

Stage 3 — SIEM investigation

Hunting queries are executed against available SIEM data. Returned events provide the evidence needed to assess whether the hypothesis is supported, contradicted, or remains inconclusive.

The presence of an IOC may justify further investigation, but does not independently establish malicious activity. Likewise, an empty result set does not prove that the activity never occurred.

Stage 4 — Evidence preservation

Investigation evidence is captured to help analysts review what was observed during a search. Evidence should be interpreted alongside the query, search window, data availability, and execution context. Screenshots support review but should not be treated as a substitute for the underlying event data.

Stage 5 — Reporting and review

The platform can consolidate available investigation information into a structured PDF report, including summaries, IOC candidates, queries, returned results, and screenshots. This provides a more consistent handoff between analysts and supports investigation documentation, peer review, and future reference.

6. Practical benefits for security teams

Reduced repetitive effort

Automates parts of intelligence extraction, query preparation, evidence collection, and reporting.

More consistent investigations

Encourages analysts to document the hypothesis, query, results, and supporting evidence in a repeatable format.

Improved reviewability

Makes investigation artefacts easier to inspect and share with authorised team members.

Detection engineering support

Helps analysts develop and review candidate SIEM queries and Sigma rules based on available intelligence.

Human-led decisions

Keeps validation, contextual interpretation, escalation, and response decisions with the security analyst.

7. Where this can help threat hunters

The value of an AI-assisted workflow is not simply generating more queries. It is helping the analyst move between intelligence, a testable hypothesis, available telemetry, and reviewable evidence with less repetitive manual work.

Turn intelligence into a starting point

Long adversary reports can be difficult to operationalise quickly. Structured summaries and IOC candidates help an analyst identify promising leads to validate.

Prepare hunts more consistently

Candidate queries and detection rules can provide a starting point for investigation, while the analyst checks syntax, field names, data availability, and coverage.

Make results easier to review

Keeping the executed query, returned results, search context, and evidence together can make it easier for another analyst to understand what was searched and what was observed.

Improve handoffs and documentation

A structured report can help communicate findings between hunters, SOC analysts, detection engineers, and incident responders without requiring everyone to reconstruct the investigation from scratch.

Practical example: An analyst reviews an adversary report, identifies a domain and a behaviour worth investigating, prepares a candidate SIEM query, runs it against the available telemetry, and reviews the results. The report can preserve the query, observed results, and screenshot evidence for follow-up. The analyst remains responsible for deciding what the evidence means.

8. What an investigation report should help answer

A useful report should make it easier to answer practical review questions, not merely package data into a PDF.

Review questionEvidence to look forImportant limitation
What was investigated?Hunt summary, hypothesis where supplied, executed query, and search context.A summary should not claim more than the underlying evidence supports.
Which indicators were considered?IOC candidates and their types, with relevant context where available.An extracted indicator is not automatically malicious or confirmed in the environment.
What did the SIEM return?Result count and returned event data from the executed search.Results depend on query logic, permissions, data-source coverage, and the selected time range.
Can another analyst review the work?Query, execution details, screenshot evidence, and report timestamp where available.A screenshot is supporting evidence; it does not replace raw events or prove that all relevant telemetry was available.

Reports are most useful when they distinguish observed facts, analyst interpretation, and unresolved questions. If the evidence is incomplete, the report should say so rather than imply certainty.

9. Integrating automation into a real SOC workflow

AI-assisted hunting should be introduced as an analyst-support capability, with clear checks before generated content is used operationally.

  1. Review the input: confirm that the intelligence source and extracted indicators are relevant to the hunt.
  2. Validate before execution: review the query, fields, index or data source, time window, permissions, and expected search cost.
  3. Interpret the output: assess the events in context and correlate with other evidence where appropriate.
  4. Preserve the investigation: retain the query, relevant results, and evidence according to the organisation's policies.
  5. Record uncertainty: document missing telemetry, query limitations, and findings that require further investigation.

For production use, teams should also consider access control, sensitive-data handling, auditability, retention requirements, and safeguards against executing unreviewed or overly broad queries.

10. How to evaluate whether it helps

Automation should be assessed by the quality of the investigation, not just by how quickly it generates output. Useful measures to evaluate include:

  • Time required to move from a threat report to a reviewed hunting query.
  • Percentage of generated queries that pass analyst review with minimal changes.
  • Accuracy and usefulness of extracted IOC candidates.
  • Completeness of the report and evidence associated with each execution.
  • False-positive rate, missed detections, and analyst effort required to validate findings.
  • Whether another analyst can reproduce or understand the investigation from its recorded context.

These are evaluation criteria, not performance claims. Results should be measured against a baseline using representative data and analyst feedback.

11. Responsible use of AI in threat hunting

AI-generated outputs can contain incorrect assumptions, unsuitable queries, incomplete IOC extraction, or false-positive detections. Before operational use, security teams should validate:

  • Query syntax and compatibility with their SIEM environment.
  • Data-source coverage and time-range assumptions.
  • IOC accuracy and threat-intelligence context.
  • Detection logic, false-positive behaviour, and performance impact.
  • Evidence integrity, access controls, and sensitive-data handling.

Operational principle: Automation should support the analyst's reasoning—not turn unverified model output into a confirmed security finding.

12. Current scope and future direction

The current demonstration focuses on intelligence analysis, query and detection assistance, Splunk-based hunt execution, screenshot evidence, and PDF investigation reporting.

Further opportunities include richer IOC enrichment, improved result correlation, stronger evidence provenance, additional SIEM integrations, and validation workflows for generated detection logic.

These areas represent potential directions for future development rather than claims about capabilities that have already been implemented.

13. Conclusion

Effective threat hunting depends on good hypotheses, reliable telemetry, repeatable investigations, and evidence that can be reviewed.

Agentic AI can help streamline parts of this process when paired with controlled execution, clear evidence, and analyst oversight.

The goal is straightforward: reduce repetitive investigation work while helping threat hunters spend more time understanding adversary behaviour and validating security findings.
